Etherphone Guide

Etherphone is a gesture-controlled live instrument: your hands choose the notes and the expression, and nothing is played with a mouse. It is built on Gesture Synth by Eric Wei, whose chord vocabulary is the default Classic map.

Two modes

The picker at the top switches between them, and the choice is remembered.

Chords — the chord hand picks a chord and the expression hand voices it. This is what the instrument did first.
Mono — the chord hand plays one note at a time, so you can play a melody, a lead line, or a bassline.

The expression hand does the same job in both: it is always the how it sounds hand. Which side each is on is Settings → Chords hand, and each hand keeps its own gesture map.

The gesture menu

Close both hands for a moment and a menu fans out around them, so you can change a setting without reaching for the mouse. Push your hand outwards towards an option to highlight it, and hold it there — a ring fills, and the choice commits when it closes. Close both hands again to dismiss the menu without choosing anything.

Chord Hand

Tilt
Inward → Major
Outward → Minor

Fingers (Scale Degree)
These follow the selected gesture map. Classic is the vocabulary inherited from Gesture Synth; Triad and Pentatonic are smaller sets. The on-screen guide always shows the active map.

Pitch Hand

Height (Pitch)
Higher in the frame → higher note. The notes are always in the selected key, over two octaves. The on-screen guide lists them, highest at the top.

Style
Quantised snaps to the nearest note and holds it.
Glide slides continuously between notes.
Harp plucks a fresh note as your hand moves from one to the next, so a sweep across the frame plays a run.
Staccato is the same, cut short — a crisp run of separate notes rather than a ringing phrase.

Re-strike
Push the pitch hand toward the camera and the note you are holding is struck again, without moving it. The one thing the styles cannot do, because a repeat is not a change of note. Switch it off in Settings if it misfires on your camera.

Snap (Settings)
How hard a glide is pulled toward the nearest note. Off is a free slide; firm makes each note a detent you land in but can still leave. It is not quantisation — the pitch stays continuous at every setting.

The looper

Record adds a layer. It arms, counts in to the next bar line — the readout counts it down — then records for the chosen number of bars and starts looping. Press it again and you get another layer rather than a wiped one: nothing is overwritten by accident, so a misflick costs nothing.

It records what your hands meant — the chord or note, not the sound — so changing the key transposes the loop along with everything else. A layer may begin at any bar: it is rotated onto the loop's grid, so it keeps time with whatever is already playing.

Each mode can hold several layers, named Chords 1, Chords 2, Mono 1 and so on. The Layers menu lists them; choosing one hands it over, and the menu then offers Mute — drop it out and back in without stopping — Louder / Quieter, for the level, Overdub, which records another pass into that layer with it still sounding so you can play along, Rewrite, which records it again from nothing, and Clear, which throws it away. The buttons and faders under the transport do the same in one click.

Save take writes the loop as JSON — every layer, its events, its level, and the tempo and loop length it was played against — and Load reads one back, adopting the take's own grid rather than the one currently selected, because a take replayed against the wrong grid is out of phase with itself. Save WAV renders the loop offline, faster than real time and independent of the tab.

Stop keeps everything, so it can be restarted. A recording it catches part-way is closed where it is rather than discarded — the last note holds to the end of the loop — so stopping is never how a take gets lost. Only Clear, Rewrite and switching mode mid-recording discard anything.

Voice loops

Record voice… captures one pass of the loop from the microphone and plays it back as an instrument, so a beatboxed groove or a hummed line becomes something you can play. The window is aligned to the bar line, because a captured loop is audio rather than intent and cannot be nudged into place afterwards.

The microphone is requested separately from the camera, and only when you first record — so denying it leaves a playable instrument rather than a dark one. Audio is captured and played back on this device only: nothing is uploaded, and nothing is routed to your speakers, so there is no feedback to avoid and no latency to compensate for.

A voice loop is registered as an instrument, like one you load from a file. Making it a layer that loops on the transport is the next step.

Transport by fist

For speed, the transport is on a gesture rather than in the menu: close one hand and flick it. The four commands are drawn around your fist as it waits, so you can read them off without looking away.

Up records — and arms to the next bar line. Down stops, keeping the loop. Right plays it again from the top. Left clears it — immediately, with no confirmation, so be sure of the direction.

The cue tracks the flick as you make it: your path is traced from where the hand settled, a ring closes around your fist as you travel, and the command you are heading for lights up. Past about three-quarters of the way it turns white-hot — this will fire — and when it does, a ring and the command's name confirm it at your hand.

One flick is one command: open your hand before flicking again. Two closed hands held still is still the menu, but a fist that moves is the transport — so a flick wins over a menu that is still counting down.

Instruments

The picker holds every instrument: the synths, a set of wavetables, and two sampled instruments. Load Wavetable… reads a single-cycle .wav and rebuilds it as a playable table; Load Sample… reads any recording and pitches it across the keyboard. Both add the file to the picker and switch to it.
